EUK-001 / RESEARCH ARCHIVE

Probability, entropy, and claims of inevitability

Project home · Zoo audit · Evaluation protocol

The two percentages

The specimen assigns more than 99.2% to a disclosure event over 5–8 years and more than 99.87% to the continued physical existence of an alleged report. It supplies neither calculation. Those numbers are recorded as claims, not adopted as estimates.

A reproducible probability claim needs a defined event, evidence set, reference class or generative model, time origin, parameter estimates, and uncertainty. A posterior estimate also requires its prior assumptions and likelihood. Validation requires predictions on data not used to choose or fit the model. None of that is supplied for these percentages.

An illustrative countermodel

This is ordinary probability reasoning introduced by the audit. It is not a fitted JFK forecast, an EFMW derivation, or an estimate of actual probabilities.

Let E mean that the alleged evidence exists and is eventually recoverable, and let q = P(E). Conditional on E, suppose the time T to disclosure follows an exponential distribution with rate λ > 0, measured in inverse years. For t ≥ 0:

P(T ≤ t) = q (1 − e−λt)

This has the correct bounds: probability is zero at the time origin, is at most q, and approaches q as time tends to infinity. If q < 1, the model never guarantees disclosure. Even with q = 1, it does not guarantee disclosure by a specified finite date.

No value of q or λ is estimated here. With q = 1, an asserted probability of 0.992 at time t would require λt > -ln(0.008), approximately 4.8283. This is algebra describing the assumption required by the percentage, not evidence for that assumption. Fitting a rate merely to reproduce the stated percentage would be circular.

The model is deliberately elementary. A more realistic hazard might vary with law, archival discovery, destruction, institutional incentives, or digitization. Such complexity increases the need for evidence rather than authorizing precise unsupported numbers.

Selection on disclosure

The specimen selects examples because they became known. A dataset assembled only from exposed operations cannot, without additional assumptions and data, estimate the probability that all concealed operations eventually become known. Unexposed cases are not ordinary observed failures: their existence may itself be unknown. That identification problem must be stated rather than erased by a list of famous disclosures.

Observations also need a time origin. Start of an operation, first suspicion, first partial leak, official acknowledgment, and final document release are different dates. Partial release is not necessarily full historical reconstruction. Changing definitions after observing the outcome can make a forecast appear successful without testing the original prediction.

Entropy is not an archival discovery theorem

The specimen names information entropy but does not define a random variable, state space, information measure, observer, or dynamics. There is no supplied derivation from an entropy expression to public discovery of a particular manuscript. Evidence may be lost or inaccessible in a candidate model; ruling that out requires an assumption, not an invocation of the word entropy.

Likewise, “multiversal phase alignment” has no operational definition or measurement protocol in the specimen. It cannot be used as an explanatory variable until those are supplied.

Dates and scoring

If the specimen’s internal February 10, 2025 date is the time origin, its 5–8-year window means February 10, 2030 through February 10, 2033. It separately gives a 2027–2032 exposure window. These may represent different intended events, but the text does not specify an unambiguous scoring rule. They should not be silently merged or selected after outcomes appear.

As of this audit’s October 13, 2026 cutoff, the absence of a model is grounds to reject the claimed mathematical certification. It is not grounds to declare every future event already falsified. Future outcomes must be assessed against a definition fixed in advance, and one correct event would still not establish probability calibration.

Requirements for a genuine quantitative study

  1. Freeze the event definition, time origin, deadline, and what would count as nonconfirmation.
  2. Define a reference class without selecting solely on eventual disclosure.
  3. Publish the data, censoring policy, model, fitting procedure, and uncertainty treatment.
  4. Compare against explicit baseline models using independent evaluation data.
  5. Report negative outcomes, uncertainty, and model misspecification.

Until those obligations are met, the numerical language is an unsupported assertion of authority.
